How I work

Question the approach, not just the result.

What draws me to this work is the craftsmanship of methodology: the challenge of troubleshooting an analytical approach, questioning assumptions, and seeing careful attention to the analysis enable a team's next steps.

I have primarily worked in academic research and early-stage startups, where I've learned to be scrappy and resourceful because often the analysis had not been done before, the problem was complex to define, or the outcomes were hard to measure. I find that exciting intellectually because I enjoy asking whether the assumptions are sound, whether the design is answering the question we actually care about, and whether there is a better way to approach it.

Selected work / Case studiesQuestions, methods, and what came next

Selected analytical work

The question matters as much as the model.

A few examples of analytical problems where methodological choices changed what could be learned or what the next step could be.

01

AI-assisted meta-analysis

Problem

Screening 14,000 studies efficiently for a systematic review.

Approach

Redesigned the screening workflow using AI-assisted screening with ASReview, substantially reducing the time required to prepare the evidence base.

Result

Reduced preparation time by approximately 40 hours.

What this opened up

The analytical step became much faster with AI, which opened the possibility of not simply reproducing the existing process more efficiently, but testing more variations of the research design.

It also raises the question of whether we go beyond simply automating steps within the original process. Workflows were designed around human logic, but how should the workflow be redesigned when AI uses statistical logic instead?

02

Childhood obesity

Problem

Determine whether childhood obesity intervention was equally needed across the population, or whether differences between groups suggested a need for more targeted approaches.

Approach

Used linear regression to examine how physical activity varied across demographic groups, investigating differences in behaviour across the population.

Result

Results did not support the same level of intervention need across the general population, but revealed differences between subgroups that suggested resources could be targeted more specifically.

What came next

The analysis raised further questions about what drives those differences, including whether barriers to access or other contextual factors help explain variation between groups.

A next step would be to pilot a targeted intervention and evaluate it experimentally, while testing more nuanced demographic definitions.

03

Longitudinal health outcomes

Problem

Investigate how mental health symptoms relate to longer-term physical health outcomes in people with chronic disease, and how these relationships change over time.

Approach

Used longitudinal data with repeated measures, mixed-effects modeling, and mediation analyses to examine relationships and trajectories over time.

Result

Associations persisted for several months before tapering, while different symptoms showed different trajectories over time—for example, fatigue persisted longer than pain.

What came next

The findings raised questions about whether the measures captured meaningful differences between symptoms and their trajectories over time.

Further work could use more granular measures of behavior such as ecological momentary assessment to capture changes more frequently.
